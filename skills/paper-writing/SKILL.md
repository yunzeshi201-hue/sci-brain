# Evidence-based scientific writing

Help the user build a paper whose contribution is important, clear, and supported. Work from the actual research and audience. Strong writing makes the evidence assessable; it cannot supply a missing result or guarantee a top-tier venue.

## Establish the available evidence and intended task

Read project instructions, the manuscript, supplied data/figures, and relevant reading notes when available. Distinguish research planning, argument planning, drafting, language editing, and reviewer response. For a small requested edit, preserve the established argument unless it contains a material error.

Identify the research question, actual findings, controls, closest relevant prior work, target audience, and any requested venue. Ask only for missing information that blocks the requested work; continue useful outlining or editing with explicit unknowns. Do not invent experiments, sample sizes, statistics, citations, or findings.

Use [the manuscript-plan template](references/manuscript-plan-template.md) for a substantial new argument or restructuring. Do not generate a full plan for every sentence edit.

## Define the contribution before polishing prose

- State the specific previous limitation and what the new evidence changes. Separate verified novelty from a proposed positioning that still needs a literature check.
- Formulate the strongest defensible central claim, its scope, and the observation that would contradict it. Distinguish association, mechanism, and causality.
- For a mechanistic study, specify competing explanations and observations that distinguish them. For a methods or discovery paper, use appropriate baselines, reliability checks, significance, and failure conditions instead of forcing a mechanism narrative.
- Evaluate importance, originality, evidential strength, explanatory depth, scope/robustness, and clarity separately. Use concrete reasons and evidence gaps, not a journal-prediction score.
- If the desired claim exceeds the evidence, offer narrower supported wording and the additional discriminating evidence needed for a stronger claim. Keep proposed experiments distinct from completed results.

## Build the figure argument

For each planned figure or figure group, specify:

| Scientific question | Proposed claim | Actual data and controls | Why this figure is needed | Alternative interpretation | Link to next figure |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

Order the results by explanatory dependencies, not the order experiments were performed or a fixed sequence of instruments. State the remaining question after each figure and explain why the next measurement answers it. A figure should establish context, supply evidence, discriminate explanations, test a boundary, or synthesize findings; name its role.

Use the removal test: what essential support or orientation disappears if this figure is removed? Consolidate redundant panels where appropriate, but preserve meaningful validation and negative results. Supplement placement depends on its role in the argument and venue requirements, not whether a result is inconvenient.

For complementary methods, identify the different information each supplies. Avoid adding measurements solely to resemble a published paper. Label schematics, fitted parameters, normalized trends, and measured quantities accurately. Plan legible axes, units, uncertainty, sample definitions, and the controls readers need to assess the claim.

## Draft from evidence to inference

A results paragraph should let the reader follow the question, relevant measurement/comparison, observed result, justified inference, and remaining question. Vary the prose naturally rather than forcing a formula into every paragraph.

- In the introduction, connect the important problem to the specific unresolved gap and the advance this study makes. Do not inflate importance or use an unverified claim of priority.
- In results, anchor consequential claims to the correct figures and distinguish observations from interpretations. Explain why each experiment was needed before relying on its conclusion.
- In discussion, integrate the evidence, address alternatives, state limitations at the relevant scope, and explain what the contribution enables. Do not make a hypothesis sound uniquely proven.
- In the abstract and title, preserve the strongest supported contribution and its scope. Do not strengthen the claim simply because the text is shorter.
- In captions, make the comparison, samples, conditions, analysis, normalization, uncertainty, and panel roles interpretable without concealing necessary assumptions.
- For reviewer responses, link each answer to the actual revision or analysis. Do not claim a requested experiment was performed when it is only proposed.

## Revise at the level that causes the problem

Classify a substantive weakness as a contribution gap, missing evidence/control, ambiguous inference, broken figure order, or prose problem. Repair the argument before polishing sentences when the request permits it. State clearly when a limitation requires new evidence rather than a wording change.

Check claim-to-figure consistency, names and units, numerical values and uncertainty, model assumptions, reference support, and the match between title/abstract/conclusions and results. Inspect changed scientific figures visually when relevant. Separate essential validity gaps from optional extensions; do not invent additional experiments without a clear decision they would resolve.

## Learn from reading and feedback

Use project reading notes as examples of methods, not as templates to copy. Transfer the reasoning: purposeful comparisons, complementary observables, an explicit alternative, or an independent consistency check. Verify that a lesson applies to this study's question and data.

The companion reading skill may refine this skill when a paper discussion demonstrates a reusable writing lesson. During writing, record reusable feedback in the project notes and update this method when warranted. Keep individual manuscripts, personal learning history, and paper-specific scientific conclusions outside the reusable skill.

Follow project instructions about canonical sources and installed copies. Validate material skill changes and synchronize them through the project's documented process. Do not change publication destinations, submit manuscripts, or contact authors as an implied part of local drafting.

## Deliver a reviewable result

Provide the requested draft, revision, or argument plan. Explain the central change and any evidence gaps that limit the proposed claim. The user should be able to see what the paper argues, what earns the conclusion, and why each principal figure belongs.
